# Academic Review Writing Standards

Use this reference to turn a detailed eight-step analysis into publication-style, evidence-aware prose without sacrificing its critical substance.

## Evidence-preserving compression

- Keep the central research problem, claimed contributions, actual study design, decisive results, and most important strengths and weaknesses.
- Prefer one well-grounded example or metric over many near-duplicates. Retain denominators, units, baselines, model settings, significance/uncertainty and scope conditions when needed to interpret a comparison.
- Distinguish the authors' finding, what their evidence supports, and the reviewer's reasoned critique.
- An incomplete or unverified source report remains incomplete or unverified after editing. If the original paper was not accessible, inherited citations must not be described as independently verified.
- Exclude claims that cannot be justified; qualify consequential unresolved points when omission would distort the review.

## Avoid section overlap

- **Summary:** State problem, approach and headline findings without interpreting every detail.
- **Background & Contributions:** Explain gap and what is claimed as new; avoid repeating the entire result list.
- **Methodology:** Explain how design, data, assumptions and evaluation work; focus on choices that determine validity.
- **Key Results & Evidence:** Use a small number of decisive findings, with correct source pointers and conditions. Avoid repeating methodological setup unnecessarily.
- **Strengths & Limitations:** Evaluate significance and methodological/evidentiary consequences; cite or refer to already-established findings, do not re-narrate the results.
- **Overall Assessment:** Deliver the independent judgment and main remaining question, not a second strengths/weaknesses section.

## Depth without verbosity

- Explain mechanisms concretely, not only names of techniques.
- For major critiques use: **specific observation → why it matters → degree of confidence or scope**.
- Prefer integrated paragraphs and limited purposeful subheadings; avoid 1.1/1.2-style interrogatory answers.
- Keep only tables/figures that materially improve the argument; do not reproduce complete benchmark leaderboards or the detailed evidence audit.
- Avoid unsupported adjectives such as "groundbreaking," "proven," "robust," or "comprehensive."
- Briefly define essential specialized terms; assume an informed academic reader, not an absolute beginner.

## Citation and correctness checks

- Every important number, method-specific assertion, novelty claim and consequential criticism requires a source pointer when supported by the input.
- Do not invent precise page, section, table or figure numbers; use the best actually verified pointer.
- Do not turn citations to prior work appearing in the focal paper into a claim that the writer directly read those studies.
- Preserve model versions, paper versions and experimental settings when they materially affect interpretation.
- When original-source access is unavailable, say so unobtrusively in the review's source limitations.
- Never imply independent experiment reproduction without actual execution and evidence.

## Final edit checks (internal)

1. Can a reader understand the study and its contribution without consulting the detailed report?
2. Does every major criticism connect to a concrete methodological/evidentiary issue?
3. Have repeated results and overlapping conclusion paragraphs been eliminated?
4. Are the decisive quantitative or formal details retained without distortions?
5. Are important qualifications, uncertainty and verification limitations still visible?
6. Does the review remain readable as one academic argument rather than a checklist?
7. Is the requested length satisfied, with approximately 3–5 pages as the default target?

Only produce the final review, not this checklist.
